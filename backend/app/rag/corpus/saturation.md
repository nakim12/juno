---
title: Saturation Curves and Diminishing Returns
topic: saturation
source: juno_kb
credibility_tier: synthesis
---
Saturation describes how a channel's incremental effect flattens as spend
increases. The first dollars spent on a channel tend to be the most productive;
as spend grows, each additional dollar buys less incremental outcome. Marketing
mix models capture this with a nonlinear transform — commonly a Hill function or
an exponential/logistic curve — parameterized by a half-saturation point (the
spend level at which the channel reaches half of its maximum effect) and a shape
parameter controlling how sharply the curve bends.

The practical consequence is that a channel's average ROI and its marginal ROI
are different numbers. Average ROI divides total attributed outcome by total
spend. Marginal ROI is the return on the next dollar, which depends on where
current spend sits on the saturation curve. A channel can have an attractive
average ROI while its marginal ROI has already collapsed because spend is well
past the half-saturation point. Budget decisions should be driven by marginal
ROI, not average ROI.

To interpret a saturation result, compare current spend to the half-saturation
point. If current spend is far below it, the channel likely has headroom and
additional budget should be productive. If current spend is at or above it, the
channel is in diminishing-returns territory and further spend is likely wasteful
— the model may even be extrapolating beyond the range of observed data, which
is unreliable.

A common failure mode is recommending more budget for a high-ROI channel without
checking saturation. Always pair a "shift budget here" recommendation with a
statement about where that channel sits on its saturation curve.
