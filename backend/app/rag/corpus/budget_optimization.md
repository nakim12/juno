The main decision a marketing mix model informs is how to allocate a budget
across channels. The principle is straightforward: at the optimal allocation,
the marginal ROI of the last dollar is equal across all channels. If one channel
has a higher marginal return than another, moving money toward it increases
total outcome — so any imbalance in marginal returns is an opportunity.

This is why saturation curves are central to optimization. As spend on a channel
increases, its marginal ROI falls along its saturation curve. The optimizer
pushes spend into high-marginal-return channels until their marginal returns
decline to meet the others. The result is usually not "put everything in the
highest-ROI channel," because that channel saturates; the optimum spreads budget
to keep every channel's marginal return balanced.

Real optimizations add constraints: minimum and maximum spend per channel,
contractual commitments, diminishing data reliability far outside the observed
spend range, and the desire not to make abrupt week-over-week changes.
Crucially, an optimizer should not extrapolate confidently beyond the range of
historically observed spend, because the saturation curve is least trustworthy
there. Recommendations to dramatically increase a channel's spend should be
staged and validated rather than executed in one step.

When translating a model into budget advice, always express recommendations in
terms of marginal returns and headroom, note where each channel sits on its
saturation curve, and stage large moves behind validation.
